🧩 How days are given out

For students, in three sentences

Put the days in your real order of preference — your favorite at the top.

After sign-ups close, everyone gets a day as high on their own list as possible; when more people want a day than it has places, a fair random draw decides.

Ranking early doesn't help, and ranking a day higher than you really want it never helps you.

“I really can't do this day”

Use it only for a real conflict. You won't be put on a day you marked — ever. If every day you can do fills up, your instructor decides where you go, with your note in front of them — nobody else is moved automatically to make room, so marking days you could really do never helps. Notes are seen only by your instructor.

Is it fair?

  • Not first-come, first-served. When you ranked isn't used at all.
  • Ties are broken by a random draw, made once for each sign-up sheet. Your place in it is fixed — it doesn't change when classmates rank, change their minds, or mark days. The same rankings always give the same schedule, so the draw can't simply be re-run until someone likes the result. (Your instructor picks how ties are broken before making the schedule, and can move people by hand afterwards — every change is kept in the sheet's history.)
  • Honesty is your best strategy. Changing your order can't get you a better day, and can't-do marks never move anyone else — they're read by your instructor.

Common questions

Why didn't I get my first choice?
Places are limited. If more students want a day than it has places, the random draw decides who gets it — then everyone else gets their next choice that still has room.

Should I put a day I don't want first, to “game” it?
No. Ranking your honest order is always at least as good as anything else, no matter what anyone else does.

When will I know my day?
When your instructor publishes the schedule, your day appears on your class's page, where you ranked.

For the curious: what the computer actually does

Picture a very polite round of speed dating between students and days. Each student “asks” their favorite day. If a day gets more requests than it has places, it holds on to some students (by its random draw, since days don't have opinions) and turns the rest away. Anyone turned away asks their next favorite day. This repeats until nobody is turned away. A day never locks anyone in until the very end — that's why it's called deferred acceptance. It's the same family of methods some cities use to assign students to public schools with limited seats.

A small example: 4 students and 2 days, with 2 places each.

  • Ana, Ben and Cleo rank Day 1 first, then Day 2.
  • Dev ranks Day 2 first, then Day 1.
  • Day 1's random draw came out Ben, Ana, Cleo, Dev.

Round 1: Ana, Ben and Cleo all ask Day 1, which has 2 places — by its draw it keeps Ben and Ana and turns Cleo away. Dev asks Day 2, which keeps Dev. Round 2: Cleo asks Day 2, which still has room. Done: Day 1 has Ana and Ben; Day 2 has Cleo and Dev. Cleo didn't get a first choice, but no change would help Cleo without bumping someone the draw put ahead.
