Days you can't make

Mark “I really can't do this day” only for a real conflict. You'll never be given a day you marked. If every day you can do is full, your instructor picks one for you, with your note in front of them. Nobody else is moved to make room, so marking a day you could do doesn't help. Only your instructor sees notes.

Technical details: the assignment algorithm

Schedules are produced with deferred acceptance (the Gale–Shapley algorithm, extended to days with several seats), the same method used by many school districts to assign students to schools with limited places. It proceeds in rounds:

  1. Each student applies to the highest day on their list that has not yet declined them.
  2. Each day holds the applicants it has room for, in the order set by its random draw, and declines the rest. No hold is final until the process ends.
  3. Declined students apply to their next choice, and the process repeats until no student is declined.

The result is stable: no student prefers a day that would have admitted them ahead of someone placed there. It is also strategy-proof for students, which is why honest ranking is the best approach (the “Most first choices” option is the exception: it is not strategy-proof).

Example. Four students and two days, with two seats each:

  • Ana, Ben, and Cleo rank Day 1 first, then Day 2.
  • Dev ranks Day 2 first, then Day 1.
  • Day 1's random draw orders the students Ben, Ana, Cleo, Dev.

In the first round, Ana, Ben, and Cleo apply to Day 1, which has two seats; following its draw, it holds Ben and Ana and declines Cleo. Dev applies to Day 2 and is held. In the second round, Cleo applies to Day 2, which still has room. Final schedule: Day 1, Ana and Ben; Day 2, Cleo and Dev. Cleo did not receive her first choice, but no change could give it to her without displacing a student the draw placed ahead of her.
